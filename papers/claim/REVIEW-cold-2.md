### Is the method valid? (Assumption (A) and the union bound in `block_on_log.py`)

**No, on both counts: the union bound is invalid as an anytime-valid test, and `block_on_log.py` departs from Assumption (A) and Proposition 3 in three ways.**

1. **The union bound $L/\alpha = 6/\alpha = 120$ is invalid because $L$ is a post-hoc random variable (`block_on_log.py:78–92, 118`).**
   In Henzi & Ziegel [2022] (cited on line 183), the forecast lag $h$ is a fixed, pre-specified integer, partitioning sittings deterministically into $h$ fixed lag classes. In `block_on_log.py`, lag classes are constructed greedily online from the realised resolution timestamps `done = max(e["rts"] for e in sittings[c])`: a new class is spawned whenever all existing classes still have an unresolved sitting (`d >= c`). Thus the total number of classes $L = \text{len(classes)}$ is a path-dependent **random variable** (the maximum number of simultaneously unresolved sittings on the sample path). Applying Ville's inequality with a threshold $L(\omega)/\alpha$ determined at the end of the path is invalid: in $\Pr(\sup_t W^{(\ell)}_t \ge u_\ell) \le 1/u_\ell$, the threshold $u_\ell$ for class $\ell$ must be a deterministic constant fixed when class $\ell$ begins, not a random variable depending on future resolution delays. Moreover, under optional stopping, an observer stopping at an earlier prefix when only $L_t = 2$ classes existed would use threshold $2/\alpha = 40$, whereas at seq 7809 the threshold is $6/\alpha = 120$.
   *Fix:* Either pre-commit a hard cap $L_{\max}$ before the window opens, or allocate $\alpha$ across dynamically spawned classes $\ell = 1, 2, \dots$ via a deterministic summable schedule $\sum_{\ell=1}^\infty \alpha_\ell = \alpha$, such as $\alpha_\ell = \frac{\alpha}{\ell(\ell+1)}$, which assigns class $\ell$ the deterministic threshold $u_\ell = \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{\alpha}$ ($40$ for class 1, $120$ for class 2, $240$ for class 3, etc.). Under that valid schedule, classes 1 and 2 ($2.15 \times 10^4 > 40$ and $1.73 \times 10^4 > 120$) still reject.

2. **Three violations of Assumption (A) and Proposition 3 in `block_on_log.py`:**
   - *(a) Post-hoc filtering to resolved events alters block membership and lag-class completion times (`block_on_log.py:48, 69–75`):* Line 183 requires *"each block's membership fixed before any of its outcomes is realised."* `block_on_log.py` takes `rows = v["rows"]` from `judge/value.py`, which includes **only events that eventually resolved**. In two sittings on 24 November (`2026-11-24T03:00:00Z` and `2026-11-24T04:00:00Z`), 6 dataset events were forecast before the cutoff, 5 resolved (`04:37:07Z` and `05:15:49Z`), and the 6th (`hk_property_transactions`, forecast seqs 7424 and 7425 with a malformed URL) never resolved and expired at `08:00:00Z` and `09:00:00Z`. Because `v["rows"]` drops unresolved events ex post, `block_on_log.py` reduces $|B_m|$ from 6 to 5 after the cutoff and computes `done` from the 5 resolved events (`04:37:07Z` and `05:15:49Z`), allowing the `05:00:00Z` and `06:00:00Z` sittings to enter lag classes 1 and 2 while the 6th event of the previous sitting in those classes was still open.
   - *(b) Grouping by window cutoff `c` rather than forecasting sitting violates forecast-time conditioning in 373 of 478 blocks (`block_on_log.py:74, 86, 102`):* Line 183 defines a sitting as *"A sitting at which several events are forecast together is one block,"* and the Remark (line 212) states that *"Assumption (A) and block betting make the null mean 'no advantage at the time of forecasting'."* In `renavondata/jack` (`transcripts/sessions/stream-forecast.md`), each forecasting sitting at hour $H$ forecasts **three consecutive hourly windows at once** (18 events across $[H+2\text{h}, H+3\text{h})$, $[H+3\text{h}, H+4\text{h})$, $[H+4\text{h}, H+5\text{h})$) at timestamp `f["ts"]`. Because `block_on_log.py` groups by window start `c = window_utc[0]` (6 events) and checks `prev_done < c` rather than `prev_done < min(f["ts"])`, in **373 of the 478 sittings** the previous sitting in the **same lag class** resolved *after* the current sitting's forecasts were already issued (`min(f["ts"]) <= prev_done < c`). While $\bar D_m$ remains adapted to the *cutoff-time* filtration $\mathcal F_c$ (since $p_{k,i}$ was fixed before $c$ and $Y_{B_m}$ resolves after $c$), `base_at(typ, c)`, `w`, and $\lambda_m$ at time $c$ use outcomes realised between `f["ts"]` and $c$ that were unknown when the forecaster issued $p_{k,i}$.
   - *(c) Cross-class and partial-sitting mixture weights (`block_on_log.py:101–107` vs. line 192):* Proposition 3 defines $w_{k,m} \propto \prod_{j<m}\prod_{i \in B_j} p_{k,i}(Y_i)$ over earlier blocks $j < m$ of that process. `block_on_log.py` instead computes `w` from all events across *all* lag classes with `e["rts"] < c`. Because 469 of the 478 sittings have multiple resolution timestamps across their 6 events (spanning up to 4 hours, e.g., sitting `2026-11-08T02:00:00Z` where 5 events resolved at `03:12:45Z..03:13:55Z` and the 6th at `07:20:23Z`), intermediate cutoffs (`04:00Z..07:00Z` on 8 Nov) update `w` on a *partial subset* (5 of 6 events) of an earlier sitting. This preserves $\mathcal F_c$-measurability of $w$ (and thus Proposition 3(a)), but breaks Proposition 3(b)'s telescoping within each lag class.

### Does the text claim more than the numbers show?

**Yes, in two major ways:**

1. **Line 367 gives a factually false explanation for why the per-event wealth ($1.5 \times 10^{60}$) drops by $10^{56}$ in block form ($2.1 \times 10^4$).**
   Line 367 asserts: *"But the per-event figure overstates the evidence by a factor of about $10^{56}$, because its bets, weights and base rates used outcomes realised in the same batch."*
   An exact ablation on `e580b64:log.jsonl` refutes this:
   - **Per-event (implementation):** $1.5198 \times 10^{60}$ (2,820 steps).
   - **Per-event with base rates, mixture weights, and stakes all frozen strictly before each sitting's cutoff `c` (zero intra-batch leakage):** **$1.5440 \times 10^{60}$** (2,820 steps) — slightly *larger*, not $10^{56}$ times smaller!
   - **Block form in a single process across all 478 sittings (no lag-class split):** **$3.5523 \times 10^{10}$** (478 steps).
   - **Block form split across the 6 lag classes (`block_on_log.py`):** **$2.149 \times 10^4$** (213 steps in class 0) and **$1.615 \times 10^4$** (194 steps in class 1).
   Thus **zero** of the $10^{56}$ drop comes from intra-batch updates of bets, weights, or base rates. Instead, $\sim 10^{50}$ of the drop ($1.54 \times 10^{60} \to 3.55 \times 10^{10}$, since $(3.55 \times 10^{10})^6 \approx 2.0 \times 10^{63}$) comes from **averaging the $\sim 6$ events per sitting into one multiplicative bet** $1 + \lambda_m \bar D_m$ (compounding once per sitting over 478 sittings instead of 6 times per sitting over 2,820 events), and the remaining $\sim 10^6$ ($3.55 \times 10^{10} \to 2.15 \times 10^4$) comes from **splitting the 478 sittings across 6 separate lag-class processes**. (Unlike Example 1, where 20 events share one coin flip $Y \sim \mathrm{Bern}(1/2)$ under a true null, each 6-dataset freshness sitting in `jack` has 3 high-frequency datasets and 3 low-frequency datasets that consistently beat the pooled $\sim 0.5$ base rate on almost every event, so per-event betting compounds positive drift 6 times per sitting.)

2. **Against a per-dataset prequential Laplace base rate, block-form wealth never exceeds $1.0$ in any lag class.**
   Of the 2,820 scored events, 2,811 are `world.renavon` freshness checks across 6 datasets (and 2,795 were forecast by a single party, `reviewer`, while `guy` and `counsel` made 0 forecasts and `builder` made 12). When `base_at(typ, c)` in `block_on_log.py` is replaced by the prequential Laplace base rate per dataset `base_ds_at((typ, dataset), c)`, the final block-form wealths across the 6 lag classes are **`[0.9975, 0.9987, 1, 1, 1, 1]`**, and the running maximum in every class is **`1.0`**. Thus 100% of the $2.1 \times 10^4$ block-form wealth comes from exploiting between-dataset base-rate heterogeneity that type-pooling ignores.

## 5. Novelty and fairness to prior work

Draft 5 is substantially fairer to prior work than Draft 4. It now properly credits autonomic computing [Kephart & Chess 2003], architecture-based self-adaptation [Garlan et al. 2004], interactive imitation learning [Ross, Gordon & Bagnell 2011; Zhang & Cho 2017], self-amending rule systems [Suber 1990; Goodman 2014], event-sourced agent logs [Nakajima 2026; Neal 2026], and recent statistical gates on self-modification [Wu et al. 2025; Shawn 2026; Sengupta 2026; Sun et al. 2026; Gao, Qi & Cui 2026]. It also explicitly disclaims mathematical novelty for Propositions 1–6 (line 125).

Two nuances should still be sharpened:
1. **Sequential comparison of forecasters under overlapping horizons:** Line 183 cites Henzi & Ziegel [2022] for splitting overlapping sittings into lag classes. In Henzi & Ziegel [2022] (and Choe & Ramdas [2024]), the lag $h$ is a deterministic horizon fixed before testing, and the e-processes across the $h$ lag classes are combined by *averaging* $\bar E_t = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{\ell=1}^h E^{(\ell)}_t$ (which is itself a valid e-process at threshold $1/\alpha$, avoiding a factor-of-$h$ union-bound penalty). The paper should note both that `block_on_log.py`'s lag classes are data-dependent (random $L$) and that averaging active lag-class e-processes (or using a pre-committed summable schedule) is strictly sharper and cleaner than an ex-post union bound.
2. **Liveness and oversight metrics:** When contrasting repair-counted liveness with prior autonomy measures (§1 line 42; §4.5 line 282), the paper should acknowledge the audit finding (`transcripts/AUDIT-criteria-20261117.md`, construction **L**) that `judge/status.py`'s liveness statistic measures only timely, repair-free pipeline cadence on self-chosen forecasts, not task difficulty or substantive work quality.

## 6. Overclaiming and clarity

## 7. Recommendation

I recommend **major revision**. The paper addresses most of the first referee's framing and literature concerns, and its core thesis and public audit log are genuinely valuable contributions to AI governance. However, the formal and empirical sections cannot stand as written while Proposition 1(b) remains false, §7 gives a demonstrably wrong causal explanation of its central empirical number and uses an invalid post-hoc random-$L$ union bound, and §4.3–§4.4 misdescribe how `judge/status.py` and `judge/judge.py` behave at `e580b64`.

The five changes that matter most, in order:

1. **Fix the §7 recomputation (`block_on_log.py`) and its interpretation (line 367):**
   - Replace the false causal explanation of the $10^{56}$ drop with the exact decomposition (freezing bets, weights, and base rates pre-cutoff gives $1.54 \times 10^{60}$; taking one bet per 6-event sitting reduces 2,820 compounding steps to 478 and gives $3.55 \times 10^{10}$; splitting into 6 lag classes gives $2.15 \times 10^4$).
   - Replace the invalid post-hoc threshold $L(\omega)/\alpha = 120$ with a valid pre-committed summable schedule $\alpha_\ell = \alpha/[\ell(\ell+1)]$ (or an average of pre-committed lag processes), fix block membership at cutoff time rather than filtering ex post to `v["rows"]` (accounting for unresolved seqs 7424–7425), and report that against a per-dataset prequential Laplace base rate the block-form peak wealth is $1.0$ in every lag class (with 2,795 of 2,820 forecasts coming from one party, `reviewer`).
2. **Fix Proposition 1(b) and its proof (lines 140, 144, 150), and qualify Proposition 1(c) (line 146):**
   - Require a competing linked log $L^*$ to start with a genesis entry (`seq = 0` or `type = "genesis"`) so that proper suffixes $L^* = (e_k, \dots, e_n)$ of $L$ are excluded and a length mismatch genuinely forces a two-line SHA-256 collision $e^*_0 \neq e_k$.
   - Note in §4.1 that `State.charter_at(i)` is currently an unused state projection in `kernel/check.py`, while `amend` entries actively constrain $a < i < k$ in the judge by forbidding `push` entries (`SPEC §10` item 12).
3. **Disclose the liveness implementation divergence in §4.3 (line 224 vs. `judge/status.py:65–119`):**
