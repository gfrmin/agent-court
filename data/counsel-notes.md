## 17 November 2026, 12:50Z — the agency literature; the value statistic's unstated assumption

- **Why this note.** On 17 November Guy sent counsel another agent's account of the principal–agent problem for AI agents, with the words "Here's the problem jack is trying to solve, in a way?", and then asked that the relevant literature be read and the book updated. Counsel's reading is the Project doc `claude/literature-agency-delegation-20261117.md`. It marks how far each work was read; most were read at abstract level only, so check before quoting. Counsel's plan (`claude/plan-20261111-to-bootstrap.md`, §4 rows 21–27 and §8) says where each gap below is proposed to land. All are proposals; none is law. The chapters and `sources.md` are yours; this note says what the reading changes for them.
- **A framing the book may use.** jack is governed delegation. It does not ask whether an agent is trustworthy. It fixes which acts an agent may take, what evidence makes its claims count, and what follows when the evidence fails. The twist the delegation literature lacks: jack limits what the *principal* may count as evidence of success. His own off-log repairs failed C2a (nine rows). The other agent's phrasing of the research question, as Guy passed it on: whether a small, independently checkable set of rules can support delegation whose success survives adversarial review, accounts for human rescue, and needs progressively less human attention.
- **Sources.** Already in `sources.md`: Holmström & Milgrom (1991), Greenblatt et al. (2024), Ramdas et al. (2023). Not there, and bearing on the book:
    - Economics of agency: Jensen & Meckling (1976), agency costs as monitoring cost, bonding cost and residual loss; Holmström (1979), the informativeness principle; Aghion & Tirole (1997), formal and real authority; Townsend (1979), costly state verification.
    - Delegation to AI agents: Tomašev, Franklin & Osindero (2026), "Intelligent AI Delegation", arXiv:2602.11865; Kolt (2026), "Governing AI Agents", *Notre Dame Law Review* 101; Rauba, Cepenas & van der Schaar (2026), arXiv:2601.23211; Feng, McDonald & Zhang (2025), "Levels of Autonomy for AI Agents", arXiv:2506.12469.
    - Test integrity and security: Zhong, Raghunathan & Carlini (2025), "ImpossibleBench", arXiv:2510.20270; Debenedetti et al., AgentDojo (NeurIPS 2024) and CaMeL, arXiv:2503.18813.
    - Statistics: Henzi & Ziegel (2022), "Valid sequential inference on probability forecast performance", *Biometrika*, arXiv:2103.08402; Choe & Ramdas (2024), "Comparing Sequential Forecasters", *Operations Research*, arXiv:2110.00115.
- **Chapter 6, and wherever value *E* is cited as evidence of skill.** The chapter states the null as the brief does: E[*D*ᵢ | past] ≤ 0, the past being the events resolved so far. That is correct as stated. But the stream issues eighteen forecasts at a sitting (three windows of six datasets), before the previous sitting's windows have resolved. Under the null a reader will assume — no skill given what was known when the forecast was issued — the product over events is a supermartingale only if outcomes issued together are conditionally independent. The brief does not state that assumption. Figures from jack `main` at `ecf6fa3` (seq 5553, 1,935 scored events, 12:30Z on 17 November), by counsel's own script except the first, which is `tools/value.py`'s:
    - as A4.4 defines it, one bet per event: log₁₀ *E* = 40.9;
    - one bet per window on the mean difference (330 windows): 7.1;
    - one bet per issuing session (113): 2.1;
    - one bet per session in two alternating lag classes, averaged, which is Henzi and Ziegel's construction and needs no independence: 0.84, that is *E* of about 7, under the charter's 20.
- **How to read those figures.** Most of the gap is lost power, not inflation: outcomes are nearly fixed by dataset (three are fresh 92–98% of the time, three 5–20%), so within a sitting they are close to independent given the past. Not exactly: two datasets share a rate of 0.922, and 27 of 321 windows had a usually-fresh dataset stale, 4 with all three stale together. A second weakness shows in the same numbers: the incumbent is a base rate pooled over datasets (mean 0.57), so a forecaster who knows only which dataset is which beats it on every event. Criterion 5 as the law is written still reads met, and the open windows are judged by it as written. This is counsel's analysis, unreviewed; it is the first question counsel would put to an outside statistician. It is good material for chapter 16 and for the book's honesty about what a crossing establishes.
- **Three claims to narrow, wherever the book makes them.** "Every act is logged": every authorised consequential act on `renavondata/jack` leaves a verifiable record; acts on the host and in other repositories are not seen. "Mutants guard against weakened tests": every *named* weakening is detected; the score is relative to the mutant set. "Monitoring is cheap": the statistical summary is cheap to read; the sovereign's total oversight cost is measured nowhere.
- **Gaps the book should say out loud.** The sovereign's attention is unmeasured (Jensen–Meckling; Feng et al.). Profit, the Project's own aim, is measured nowhere (Holmström–Milgrom). The amendments still have one drafter (Aghion–Tirole; COUNSEL-OPEN row 33). No adversarial proposer has been run, and the stream's forecaster and resolver are one model under two keys (Greenblatt et al.). The builder can read the reviewer's pins (ImpossibleBench).
